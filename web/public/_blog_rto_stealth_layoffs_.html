Return-to-Office Mandates are Triggering a New Era of Workplace Sabotage

72% of workers suspect RTO is a stealth layoff—here’s how they’re weaponizing their presence.

Written by Doroteya Vasileva, CPRW

Senior Content Writer & Editor

Published: 3/9/2026•

The corporate world is currently locked in a high-stakes psychological standoff. While CEOs champion the office as a hub for "serendipitous innovation" and "culture-building," the view from the cubicle looks radically different. New data suggests that the push to bring employees back to the desk has triggered a profound breach of the employer-employee social contract.

Rather than fostering collaboration, strict return-to-office (RTO) mandates have given rise to The Great Compliance—a phenomenon where workers show up physically to satisfy badge-trackers while mentally and strategically checking out.

From coffee badging to job hunting at the desk, employees are finding ways to weaponize their presence.

To get a clearer picture of this shift, we surveyed 1,000 full-time workers across the United States who have been subject to new or stricter RTO policies within the last 12 months.

Here’s how they’re responding to the mandatory return.

RTO mandates by the number
  • A massive trust deficit: The average trust score for RTO motives sits at a mediocre 59/100.
  • The stealth layoff theory: 72% of employees believe mandates are a deliberate tool to drive voluntary attrition and avoid severance.
  • A performance of attendance: 46% of workers admit to coffee badging (swiping in and leaving early), a tactic that becomes the norm among those who suspect a hidden corporate agenda.
  • Strategic exit planning: Over one in three employees are now using their time at the office to apply for new jobs or build side hustles as a financial safety net.

Is RTO just a stealth layoff in disguise?

Corporate leadership often frames the return to the office as a necessary step for mentorship and innovation. However, our data reveals a significant disconnect between management's talking points and employee reality.

When asked why they believe these mandates are being implemented, workers didn't point to culture. They pointed to the bottom line.

A staggering 72% of surveyed employees suspect their company’s RTO mandate is, in fact, attrition by design—a stealth layoff intended to make work life inconvenient enough that people quit voluntarily, allowing the company to reduce headcount without the PR nightmare or financial burden of severance packages.

This isn't just a fringe theory held by a few disgruntled workers. Even among the remaining 28% who didn't explicitly call it a stealth layoff, trust remains incredibly low. On a scale of 0 to 100, the average score for how much employees trust that RTO is genuinely about productivity rather than reducing headcount is just 59.

When workers believe the culture narrative is a cover for a cost-cutting agenda, the psychological contract is effectively canceled.

When asked to write a hypothetical, anonymous message to their CEO, one respondent captured the prevailing sentiment perfectly:

“I would have to ask which they wanted more: butts in the office or productivity? For a very dedicated employee who has been there for 16 years, this was very much a slap in the face.”

The recurring themes are clear: productivity is down, stress is up, and the "culture" being built is one of resentment.

“Spending almost four hours commuting a day is not a good use of my time. My entire time working at home, I have worked 10-12 hours a day. Now, I can only work eight hours because of the drive. I am more stressed, and I know I am not as productive.”

Whether or not these mandates are officially intended as layoffs is almost secondary to the data: because 72% of workers believe they are, the 'culture' CEOs are trying to build is already being replaced by a strategy of self-preservation.

From compliance to resistance: The rise of tactical presence

Once employees feel they’re being pushed out or misled, their internal monologue shifts from "How can I contribute?" to "How can I protect myself?" Our survey captured the specific ways this tactical presence manifests.

The most visible trend is coffee badging—where nearly half the workforce (46%) admits to showing up just long enough to swipe their badge and be "seen" before leaving to work elsewhere.

While leadership often turns to strict badge-tracking to ensure productivity, our data suggests that aggressive monitoring actually backfires. We’ve identified a surveillance paradox: the more a company tracks a worker, the less that worker believes in the company’s mission.

This collapse in trust turns the office into a liability. When trust hits the floor, resistance goes deeper than badging. For many, the office has now become the primary location for their next job search.

  • 36% of surveyed employees have applied for a new job while sitting at their current office desk.
  • 32% admit to intentionally reducing their daily output as a form of quiet protest.
  • 36% have started a side hustle since the mandate was announced, seeking "extra security" in an environment they no longer trust.

The irony for leadership is clear: the very desks they fought to fill are now being used as staging grounds for the Great Exit.

Diversification of loyalty: Why fairness matters

A significant shift in employee behavior suggests that long-term retention is now a major business risk. Our data reveals that the RTO mandate hasn't just changed where people work—it’s changed who they work for. Rather than focusing exclusively on their primary roles, many workers are now hedging their bets by diversifying their income and loyalty.

This shift is driven by a perceived negotiation gap. While back-to-office mandates are often framed as a one-size-fits-all cultural reset, our research shows the reality is far more fragmented.

27.8% of interviewed employees have successfully negotiated a personal exception to the policy, while the rest are left to comply. This creates a visible two-tier workforce, where those without the leverage to opt out feel uniquely targeted.

When you combine this lack of equity with the fact that 72% of surveyed employees suspect these mandates are actually stealth layoffs in disguise, the result is a total collapse of traditional corporate loyalty. If the company is perceived to be using office requirements to trigger resignations, employees no longer feel a moral obligation to give 100% of their energy to a single employer.

The data shows this is an economic strategy:

36% of surveyed employees report starting a side hustle or secondary income stream specifically for extra security since the mandate was announced.

In the eyes of the modern professional, the office has become a place to secure their own professional and financial futures—often using company time to do it.

"This policy has quietly reshaped how I see my future here. What was once a place I believed in has become just a place I work... Loyalty isn't demanded—it's earned through actions that show you see us as people, not just roles."

А respondent describes the fear driving the need for a safety net in exact numbers:

"Just because other companies have RTO mandates doesn’t mean we need to, especially when we have zero amenities, and it costs $250/month to park in the building.”

The mentorship myth: A generational trust gap

While many CEOs justify RTO mandates as a necessary tool for mentoring the next generation, our data suggests a profound disconnect. Gen Z (ages 18–24) and younger Millennials reported the lowest trust in corporate motives, with average trust scores nearly 20 points lower than their Boomer counterparts.

When the friction of a mandated return meets the suspicion of a hidden attrition agenda, the feedback is visceral.

“The idea that we need to be present is asinine. Many come in just to socialize and then go home. No more work is getting done here than at home.”

For this cohort, the collaboration narrative is viewed with extreme skepticism. They’re the group most likely to believe RTO is a stealth layoff tool. By forcing a physical presence that this generation views as performative, companies may be solving a short-term real estate problem while creating a long-term retention crisis with the very talent they claim to be developing.

Survey methodology

To ensure the integrity of this data, we surveyed 1,000 full-time employees across the United States who have been subject to a new or stricter return-to-office policy within the last 12 months.

  • Platform: Research was conducted via Pollfish, using a double-opt-in, mobile-first survey methodology.
  • Filter: Only respondents who confirmed they are currently employed full-time and have experienced a mandatory RTO shift were permitted to complete the survey.
  • Questions: Participants were asked to rate their trust in corporate motives, identify specific workplace behaviors, and provide an anonymous, hypothetical message to their leadership regarding the impact of these policies.
  • Tone of response: All open-ended responses were provided anonymously to encourage the highest level of candor and transparency.
